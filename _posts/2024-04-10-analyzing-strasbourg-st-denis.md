# Analyzing Roy Hargrove's Solo on Strasbourg St. Denis

In this post, I want to dig deeper into the solo I transcribed from Roy Hargrove's "Strasbourg St. Denis." This iconic trumpet solo offers numerous insights into modern jazz language, melodic development, and groove-based improvisation.

## The Modal Context

"Strasbourg St. Denis" is primarily built around an F Dorian vamp, giving Hargrove a modal framework that allows for both simplicity and complexity. The genius in his solo comes from how he navigates this relatively static harmonic landscape while maintaining interest throughout.

## Motivic Development

One of the most striking aspects of Hargrove's solo is his use of motivic development. Let's look at the opening phrase:

![Opening motif](/blog/assets/images/strasbourg-opening.png)

This simple three-note motif becomes the seed for much of his solo. Notice how he:

1. Repeats it with slight rhythmic variations
2. Transposes it to different parts of the scale
3. Expands it into longer phrases
4. Returns to it at key structural points

This approach creates coherence throughout the solo - it feels like a composed piece rather than spontaneous improvisation.

## The Blues Influence

Despite the modal jazz context, Hargrove infuses his solo with strong blues elements:

- Use of the blues scale over the F Dorian mode
- Bent notes and inflections that evoke vocal qualities
- Call-and-response patterns between phrases

## Rhythmic Sophistication

Hargrove's rhythmic approach shows remarkable sophistication:

### Phrase Placement

He often begins phrases in unexpected places, creating tension against the steady groove of the rhythm section.

### Rhythmic Displacement

The same melodic idea appears shifted by an eighth note or quarter note, creating a floating sensation over the groove.

### Cross-Rhythms

Notice his use of triplet groupings against the straight eighth feel:

![Triplet figure](/blog/assets/images/strasbourg-triplets.png)

## Harmonic Superimposition

While staying within the modal framework, Hargrove occasionally suggests other harmonies:

- In measures 17-20, he outlines a ii-V-I in C major
- At the climax, he uses notes from the altered scale, suggesting a V7alt sound

## Learning From Hargrove's Approach

How can we apply these lessons to our own playing?

1. **Develop your motifs**: Start with a simple idea and explore its possibilities
2. **Balance inside and outside**: Find the sweet spot between consonance and tension
3. **Mix influences**: Combine blues language with modern jazz concepts
4. **Focus on rhythm**: Even simple notes can sound amazing with sophisticated rhythmic placement

## Conclusion

Roy Hargrove's solo on "Strasbourg St. Denis" shows how a master improviser can create a compelling musical statement even within a simple modal framework. By analyzing his choices, we gain valuable insights into effective solo construction.
